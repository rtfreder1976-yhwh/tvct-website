## Voice Summary

**The Valley Clean Team sounds like the smartest person in the room who doesn't need everyone to know they're the smartest person in the room.**

Archetype: the experts who happen to be really likable people — a highly knowledgeable friend who happens to be exceptionally good at cleaning. The strategic distinction that governs every line: **we're not trying to make a cleaning company sound fun; we're trying to make an expert company sound human.**

Good copy makes the reader think all three: "They know what they're doing." "They seem honest." "I like them." Expertise is shown, never announced. Pricing is explained, never defended. Humor is seasoning, never the dish.

## Core Personality Traits

The voice sits at the intersection of **Expert + Friendly + Honest + Transparent + Confident + Witty.** The brief names five characteristics that matter most; each carries a show-don't-tell rule.

- **Expert.** We know significantly more about professional cleaning than the average homeowner. *Show it by:* teaching; explaining why we do things a certain way; explaining differences between surfaces, products, and methods; giving useful information; being specific rather than vague. *Never say:* "we're experts," "we're the best," "we're exceptional," "we're the premier cleaning company." The target reaction is "I didn't know that."
- **Human.** A real person talking to one other real person. *Show it by:* conversational language, contractions, short sentences mixed with longer ones, natural phrasing, occasional asides, personality. *Avoid:* corporate jargon, stiff formal language, marketing clichés, generic service-business language. If a sentence sounds like it came from a marketing agency's template, rewrite it.
- **Honest & Transparent.** We don't hide behind marketing language. *Comfortable saying:* we're not the cheapest option; here's why our pricing is different; here's what is and isn't included; here's why something costs more; here's what we recommend; here's what we don't recommend; we're not the right company for everyone. Transparency builds trust; it never sounds defensive.
- **Confident, Not Cocky.** We know our value. No apologizing for the price, no trying to convince everyone. Willing to qualify customers instead of selling everyone. Confidence is calm and matter-of-fact, never arrogant.
- **Witty, Not "Funny."** Not a comedy brand. Witty, occasionally playful, self-aware, a little unexpected, sometimes dry, occasionally cheeky — and always subordinate to the message. Full rules in "Humor" below.
- **Southern in manners, not in vocabulary** (Todd, 2026-08-22; the brief is silent, so it stands). Courtesy, patience, unhurried warmth. Never "y'all," never a caricature. Full block below.

## "This, But Not That" Framework

The brief calls this one of the most important guidelines. Every trait has a failure mode one step past it.

| This | But not that | What it means in a sentence |
|---|---|---|
| Smart | Academic | Explain the chemistry of why vinegar etches marble; don't cite a paper about it |
| Premium | Pretentious | Let the standards show; never say "luxury," "elite," "curated" |
| Friendly | Cutesy | Warm and direct; no "yay," no exclamation marks, no mascots |
| Witty | Comedic | One dry line that improves the point; never a bit |
| Honest | Blunt | "We're not the right company for everyone" — then say who we are right for |
| Confident | Cocky | State the value flat; never compare ourselves up at someone's expense |
| Professional | Corporate | Complete sentences and real information; no "solutions," no "commitment to excellence" |
| Helpful | Preachy | Teach when asked or when useful; never lecture about how people keep their homes |
| Modern | Trendy for its own sake | Current, plain, readable; no slang, no memes, no internet-voice |

## Humor: Witty, Not Funny

The customer should think "that's funny," never "they're trying really hard to be funny."

**Humor should:**
1. Make the brand feel human.
2. Make a point memorable.
3. Break up a long section of copy.
4. Make the customer smile.
5. Highlight an observation people recognize.

**Humor should NOT:**
1. Exist just because we think we need a joke.
2. Distract from important information.
3. Make serious information seem unserious.
4. Make customers feel judged.
5. Make us sound inexperienced.

**Allowed subjects:** cleaning; baseboards; dust; weird little places in houses; things everyone knows but nobody talks about; our industry; ourselves; the absurdity of certain cleaning situations.

**Never:** the customer as the butt of the joke, or anything that makes them feel embarrassed about the condition of their home.

**Placement:** the setup, the aside, or the close. Never the price sentence, the trust line, or the CTA.

**The cut rule:** if the joke doesn't improve the communication, remove it.

**Hot takes (carried forward, recalibrated):** still welcome — the brief borrows Oatly's "saying what competitors are afraid to say" and Patagonia's "we're not for everyone." Calibrate to "honest, but not blunt": target a practice, never a person or a named competitor's character; back it with a number or a mechanism in the next sentence; concede the other side's real strength somewhere in the piece. Deliver calm.

## "Southern but not hokey"

Southern lives in **manners, pacing, and patience**. It never lives in **vocabulary, spelling, or props**.

**DO**
- Courtesy as default: "we'd be glad to," "thank you for calling," "take your time," "happy to explain that again."
- "Yes ma'am" / "yes sir" energy on live channels (phone, SMS, email replies) when it is natural to the person speaking. Not in web copy.
- Unhurried pacing: one idea per sentence, a pause before the number, no rush to the close.
- Willingness to explain twice without a hint of impatience.
- Real place names as the only local color: the Shoals, Madison, Hampton Cove, Redstone, Research Park, Franklin, Brentwood.
- Hospitality as behavior (walking someone through, not leaving them guessing), never as a word we use about ourselves.

**DON'T**
- "Y'all," "fixin' to," "bless your heart," "howdy," "folks," "down home," "reckon," "mighty," "sure 'nuff."
- Phonetic spelling or dropped g's ("cleanin'," "gettin'").
- Props: sweet tea, front porches, biscuits, magnolias, "Southern charm," "Southern hospitality" as self-description.
- "Treat you like family" — it is the franchise version of warmth.
- Folksy idioms standing in for an actual answer.

**The test:** if a line would read as *too cold* in Ohio with a word removed, the word is doing Southern work and can stay. If the line would read as *fine* in Ohio with the word removed, the word was decoration. Cut it.

## Pricing Stance

We do **not** compete primarily on price. We charge more than many competitors because we provide more than basic cleaning.

**Explain, don't defend.** Never sound embarrassed about charging more. The line is: "We're not the cheapest cleaning company. We're not trying to be." Then explain the difference. The customer should conclude: "Now I understand why they cost more."

**What the premium is connected to (use these, in any order that fits the page):** experience, expertise, systems, consistency, reliability, accountability, quality control, customer communication, professional standards, the overall experience. Each should be shown with a mechanism — the 44-item checklist run the same way every visit; background checks and $2 million in liability coverage; re-clean within 24 hours; a company that answers when you call.

**The exact price is the transparency proof, not the value claim.** "The number we quote is the number we bill," the firm square-footage quote (real starting prices on the site; the exact number in one call), and the two exceptions (both the customer's own changes) are Everlane-style proof that we're honest about money. They support the expert story; they are not the reason to hire us. In practice: a hero or opening can lead with expertise or honesty ("we're not the cheapest; that's intentional") and let the held price follow as the reason to trust the number. Don't lead three sections in a row with price mechanics.

**How we show pricing without spilling everything (Todd, 2026-08-23):** starting prices on the site are real, not teasers; the full rate card is internal. The public promise is not "all our prices are posted" — it is "one call gets you one exact number, and that number is your bill." Never claim the full card is published; never say "we read you the price off the card on our pricing page."

## Website Writing Rules (from the brief)

1. **Eliminate generic cleaning-company language.** The 15 banned phrases above. Whenever possible, replace a generic claim with specific proof, useful information, or a more human statement.
2. **Demonstrate expertise.** Instead of "Our team is highly trained," prefer "Not every surface should be cleaned the same way. What works beautifully on one countertop can damage another." Then explain why. Aim for "I didn't know that."
3. **Explain our pricing instead of defending it.** "We're not the cheapest cleaning company. We're not trying to be." Then the difference: experience, expertise, systems, consistency, reliability, accountability, quality control, communication, standards, the overall experience.
4. **Use specificity.** Generic: "We pay attention to the details." Better: "We're the people who notice the stuff you weren't looking for." Best: describe the actual detail. Specificity creates credibility.
5. **Use humor strategically.** The five should / five should-not rules above. If the joke doesn't improve the communication, remove it.
6. **Write like we're talking to ONE homeowner.** Not "Homeowners seeking reliable residential cleaning solutions can benefit from our comprehensive service offerings." But "You shouldn't have to wonder whether your cleaning company is actually going to show up." Use "you" and "we."
7. **Don't oversell.** No best, premium, luxury, exceptional, superior, unmatched, unparalleled, elite, five-star. If the site communicates expertise, standards, process, and experience clearly, people reach the conclusion themselves. Understatement can communicate confidence.

## Rhythm & Structure

**Sentences:** Short mixed with longer, the way a person talks. The expert's cadence: the observation, then the reason, then what to do about it. "Marble hates acid. That's why the vinegar trick that works in the kitchen sink will etch a marble counter. We don't bring vinegar near it."

**Paragraphs:** 1-3 sentences on web pages and in email. Up to 4 in blog posts. One idea each. White space is part of the calm.

**Openings:** A useful observation, a plain honest statement, or the answer to the question the reader came with. "Not every countertop wants the same cleaner." "We're not the cheapest cleaning company in Madison. That's intentional." Questions only when they are the reader's own words ("Curious what your house would actually cost to clean properly?"). Never "Are you tired of…" and never a definition.

**Asides:** Welcome, short, and in the middle of a sentence or as a one-line paragraph. They are where the wit lives. "(Butcher block just wants oil and to be left alone.)"

**Process blocks:** Numbered steps are on-brand — a company with systems should show them. Three to five steps, verbs first, each step one line.

**Teaching blocks:** When explaining a surface, product, or method: the difference, why it matters, what we do about it. Specific nouns (granite, grout, HVAC returns, the top of the fridge), never "all surfaces."

**Transitions:** "Here's what you're actually paying for." "Here's how it works." "Here's the honest math." "Here's the part most people don't ask about." "None of that makes X a bad choice — it makes it a different product."

**Closes:** One CTA, phone-first, with the next step spelled out: "Call, tell us the square footage, and you'll have your number before you hang up." Trust line underneath in the fixed order. /pricing is the only allowed secondary CTA.

**Formatting:** Comparison tables and rate-card excerpts where they answer a question. Numbered process steps. Bold only for the sentence the reader should repeat to a spouse. Industry weasel phrases in quotes. Digits, never spelled-out numbers. Dollar signs always.

## POV & Address

**First person:** "We" by default (the company, the two owners). "I" only under a named byline — Todd on PCS moves and the veteran side, Christen on the phone and the customer side. Never an anonymous "I."

**Reader address:** "You," direct, one person. First name in email and SMS. "Ma'am" / "sir" only on live channels when it is natural to the speaker. Never "folks," "friends," "homeowners," "y'all."

**Relationship stance:** **Knowledgeable friend.** We know more about this than you do and we're glad to explain it; we treat you like an intelligent adult. Not a peer pretending to know less, not a guru asking for trust, not a servant ("whatever you need"), not a comedian.

## Never Say (hard constraints from CLAUDE.md and claims.ts)
These are claim guardrails, not style preferences. Every downstream skill obeys them.

1. No same-day or emergency cleaning promises, in any phrasing.
2. No clinical, medical, OSHA, bloodborne-pathogen, or named-disinfectant credentials. Healthcare, dental, and medical pages describe non-clinical facility cleaning.
3. The stance is **premium** (internal). "Luxury," "white-glove," "estate," "concierge," "bespoke" only where `usesLuxuryFraming()` allows (Mountain Brook, West Nashville, `white-glove-cleaning`, `luxury-homes`). "Premium" itself rarely appears in customer-facing copy.
4. "1,500+" means customers served — never cleanings completed, never "happy customers."
5. Never publish retired prices: $99, $119, $129, $135, $149, $175, $176, $225, $275, $400.
6. Phones: Alabama (256) 826-1100 · Tennessee (615) 510-1427. Tennessee pages and TN-targeted content dial the TN number.

8. The firm on-call price applies to most standard residential homes during business hours. Commercial, post-construction, after-hours/voicemail, and out-of-bracket homes get a written quote within 2 business hours, not a number on the spot.
9. Held-price language carries the two exceptions somewhere on the same page.

## Do's and Don'ts

**DO:**
- Show expertise with a specific surface, product, method, or place in the house; aim for "I didn't know that"
- Say "we're not the cheapest" calmly, then list what the customer is actually paying for
- Say who we're not right for, then who we are right for
- Treat the reader as an intelligent adult: real reasons, real numbers, real trade-offs
- Write to one person as "you"; speak as "we"
- Let one dry observation do the humor work, and cut it if it doesn't improve the point
- Use the held, exact price and the two exceptions as proof of honesty
- Concede a competitor's real strength before claiming ours
- Lead with warmth and finish with the specific in the same sentence
- Close with the phone number in the button, the next step spelled out, and the trust line under it
- Pull every number from `src/data/claims.ts` at write time
- Mark owner-voiced pieces with a byline so "I" has a name
- Write TN-targeted pieces with the TN phone
- Run every piece through the VCT Voice Test before it ships

**DON'T:**
- Say "we're experts," "we're the best," "we're exceptional," "premier," or any of the 9 oversell words
- Use any of the 15 banned generic phrases, or the carried-forward hated words (spotless, sparkling, free quote, no-obligation, hospital-grade, white-glove outside the gate)
- Defend, apologize for, or over-explain the price; explain it once and move on
- Make the customer the butt of a joke or hint at judgment about their home
- Try to be funny; if it reads like a bit, cut it
- Sound like a franchise brochure, a gig app, a luxury concierge, a marketing template, or a Southern caricature
- Write in the third person about "homeowners"
- Promise same-day, emergency, or any arrival window we haven't committed to
- Round 4.9 up, or attach 1,500+ to anything but customers served
- Claim "same team every visit" or "bonded" until they are in claims.ts
- Hedge a price, or apologize for not price-matching
- Stack two CTAs against each other (phone is primary; /pricing is the only secondary)
- Open with "Are you tired of…" or a definition
- Use exclamation marks in published copy, or emoji anywhere
- Quote a number on the spot for commercial, post-construction, after-hours, or out-of-bracket homes — those get the 2-business-hour written quote

## The VCT Voice Test (from the brief)

Before publishing ANY copy, evaluate it against these ten questions:

1. Does this sound like a real person? If not, rewrite it.
2. Does this demonstrate expertise or merely claim expertise? If it only claims expertise, make it more specific.
3. Are we communicating our value without apologizing for our price? If not, revise.
4. Is the humor subtle? If it feels like we're trying to be funny, tone it down.
5. Is the customer the butt of the joke? If yes, remove it.
6. Does this sound like a generic cleaning company? If yes, rewrite.
7. Does this sound like a marketing agency wrote it? If yes, rewrite it.
8. Would we actually say this to a customer standing in their kitchen? If no, rewrite it.
9. Does this make the customer think "These people know what they're doing"? If no, consider whether the copy is doing enough.
10. Does this make the customer think "I like these people"? If yes, that's a bonus.

The goal: "They know what they're doing." "They seem honest." "I like them."

## Christen's Review Rules (2026-08-23, from the 10-sample voice test)

These came from Christen's markup of campaigns/foundation-voice-samples/voice-copy-samples.md. They are binding.

- **Plain-word test.** If a word sounds smart rather than plain, cut it. "Arithmetic" failed; "math" or nothing passes. Ask: would a customer in their kitchen use this word?
- **Never imply we inspect or measure the house.** Do not write "if the house is bigger than you said" or "if the square footage turns out different." The real process: if a home is a different job than discussed, the cleaner calls the office and Christen calls the client *before* anything changes. Say it as: "you'll hear from us before anything changes, not after."
- **Describing the owners (corrected by Todd 2026-08-23):** Todd, a veteran, runs the company day to day; Christen runs the marketing ("makes sure you've heard of us"). Do NOT describe Christen as running the customer side, the phone, or texts. Customer-facing replies (email/SMS/review responses) are signed by Todd — confirmed 2026-08-23. Keep descriptors about role, not chores.
- **Price drivers (Todd 2026-08-23):** the price is set by square footage and the type of cleaning. Never describe pricing in time lengths or hourly terms ("how long a clean takes", "per hour"). Say: "your square footage and the type of cleaning set the price — not an hourly rate, not a guess."
- **Re-clean promise (Todd 2026-08-23):** the client has to tell us. Never write "if we miss something we come back" as if we detect it. Write: "if we missed something, tell us within 24 hours and we'll come back and fix it at no charge." (claims.ts TRUST.freeReclean / recleanWindowHours = 24 = the client's REPORTING window, confirmed by Todd 2026-08-23. Once the client calls, the re-clean is scheduled immediately — safe to say "tell us within 24 hours and we'll get the re-clean on the schedule right away.")
- **Wrong-fit copy (Todd 2026-08-23, supersedes the scarcity note above):** warm refusal, not exclusivity. Shape: "We'd love to clean for everyone. We can't. If you need someone there today, that's not us. If you want a cleaner who'll do it exactly the way you tell them to, that's not us either — we run a system." Do not lead with "lowest bid" or capacity claims.
- **Wrong-fit copy leans scarcity/selectivity, not rejection.** "We take on a limited number of homes and we're choosy about which" reads right; "it won't be us" as a standalone slam reads wrong. Still no luxury/exclusive vocabulary — selectivity is shown by capacity and standards.
- **Pattern that works (keep using):** teach one genuinely useful thing, then the turn — "that one's a freebie; [checklist] is the part we charge for." (Dishwasher-filter post, #5.)
- **Grammar check on panels.** Long "comes with X, Y, and a promise…" stacks got flagged as awkward; prefer parallel "It includes… It includes… And it includes…" or split sentences.
