## Brand Positioning

The Valley Clean Team is a premium residential cleaning company. We charge more than many competitors because we provide more than basic cleaning: we provide expertise, experience, reliable systems, accountability, consistency, and a professional customer experience.

We do NOT want to compete primarily on price.

Our brand should communicate:

- We know what we're doing.
- We take cleaning seriously.
- We have high standards.
- We're honest about what we do and what we don't do.
- We're transparent about pricing and expectations.
- We're friendly and approachable.
- We're confident enough to occasionally be witty.
- We treat customers like intelligent adults.
- We don't need to constantly tell people we're "premium" or "exceptional." The quality of our communication should demonstrate it.

### Core positioning

We're the experts who happen to be really likable people.

Or, more specifically:

> We're trying to make an expert company sound human — not make a cleaning company sound fun.

The voice should feel like a highly knowledgeable friend who happens to be exceptionally good at cleaning.

## Brand Voice

Our ideal voice sits at the intersection of:

**Expert + Friendly + Honest + Transparent + Confident + Witty**

### The five most important characteristics

#### 1. Expert

We know significantly more about professional cleaning than the average homeowner.

Demonstrate that expertise by:

- Teaching people things.
- Explaining why we do things a certain way.
- Explaining differences between surfaces, products, methods, etc.
- Giving useful information.
- Being specific rather than vague.

Do NOT repeatedly say:

- "We're experts."
- "We're the best."
- "We're exceptional."
- "We're the premier cleaning company."

Show expertise rather than announcing it.

#### 2. Human

Our copy should sound like a real person talking to another real person.

Use:

- Conversational language.
- Contractions.
- Short sentences mixed with longer ones.
- Natural phrasing.
- Occasional asides.
- Personality.

Avoid:

- Corporate jargon.
- Stiff formal language.
- Marketing clichés.
- Generic service-business language.

If a sentence sounds like it came from a marketing agency's template, rewrite it.

#### 3. Honest & Transparent

We don't hide behind marketing language.

We're comfortable saying:

- We're not the cheapest option.
- Here's why our pricing is different.
- Here's what is and isn't included.
- Here's why something costs more.
- Here's what we recommend.
- Here's what we DON'T recommend.
- We're not the right company for everyone.

Transparency should build trust rather than feel defensive.

Example — instead of:

> "Our premium pricing reflects the exceptional quality of our services."

Say:

> "We're not the cheapest option. That's intentional."

Then explain what the customer is actually paying for.

#### 4. Confident, Not Cocky

We know our value.

Don't apologize for our pricing or try to convince everyone to choose us.

It's okay to communicate:

> "We're not the right cleaning company for everyone."

We should be willing to qualify customers instead of desperately trying to sell everyone.

Confidence should feel calm and matter-of-fact — not arrogant.

#### 5. Witty, Not "Funny"

This distinction is extremely important.

We are NOT a comedy brand.

We should be:

- Witty.
- Occasionally playful.
- Self-aware.
- A little unexpected.
- Sometimes dry.
- Occasionally cheeky.

But humor should be subtle and should never overpower the actual message.

The customer should think: "That's funny." Not: "They're trying really hard to be funny."

Never make the customer the butt of the joke.

We can joke about:

- Cleaning.
- Baseboards.
- Dust.
- Weird little places in houses.
- Things everyone knows but nobody talks about.
- Our industry.
- Ourselves.
- The absurdity of certain cleaning situations.

We should NOT joke at the customer's expense or make them feel embarrassed about the condition of their home.

## The "This, But Not That" Framework

This is one of the most important guidelines for the brand.

- Smart, but not academic.
- Premium, but not pretentious.
- Friendly, but not cutesy.
- Witty, but not comedic.
- Honest, but not blunt.
- Confident, but not cocky.
- Professional, but not corporate.
- Helpful, but not preachy.
- Modern, but not trendy for the sake of being trendy.

## Brand References / Inspiration

These brands are reference points for specific aspects of the voice. Do NOT copy their personality, language, slogans, or aesthetic.

**Mailchimp** — Borrow: approachable expertise; explaining complicated things simply; conversational educational content; subtle humor; speaking with customers rather than down to them. Do not borrow: tech-company quirks; excessive cleverness.

**Oatly** — Borrow: radical honesty; self-awareness; saying what competitors are afraid to say; confidence; humor underneath the message rather than humor replacing the message. Do not borrow: absurdity; aggressive irreverence.

**Innocent Drinks** — Borrow: human conversational language; warmth; small moments of personality; playful observations. Do not borrow: childishness; excessive whimsy.

**Everlane** — Borrow: pricing transparency; explaining what customers are actually paying for; premium positioning without apologizing for price. Do not borrow: fashion/luxury language.

**REI** — Borrow: expert friend energy; useful advice; knowledge without condescension; "we're on your side."

**Trader Joe's** — Borrow: personality in small pieces of copy; unexpected phrasing; human language; making ordinary interactions feel personal.

**Aesop** — Borrow: restraint; confidence; premium positioning without constantly saying "luxury"; intelligent language.

**Liquid Death** — Borrow ONLY: the willingness to say something unexpected; the confidence to break category conventions. Do NOT borrow: aggressive humor; shock value; their overall personality.

**Patagonia** — Borrow: conviction; being willing to say "we're not for everyone"; having standards and opinions; honest communication even when it isn't the easiest sales pitch.

**Method** — Borrow: proof that a traditionally boring cleaning category can have personality; modern, human communication.

**1-800-GOT-JUNK?** — Borrow: the idea that a home-service company doesn't have to sound like a traditional home-service company; memorable personality; turning a mundane service into a recognizable brand.

## Writing Rules for the Website

### 1. Eliminate generic cleaning-company language

Avoid phrases like:

- "We make your home sparkle."
- "Sit back and relax."
- "We treat your home like our own."
- "Exceptional cleaning services."
- "Unparalleled service."
- "Attention to detail."
- "Customer satisfaction is our top priority."
- "Locally owned and operated."
- "Your satisfaction is guaranteed."
- "We go above and beyond."
- "Take back your time."
- "Let us do the dirty work."
- "A cleaner, healthier home."
- "We bring the sparkle."
- "Professional cleaning you can trust."

These aren't necessarily bad statements, but they're overused and don't differentiate us. Whenever possible, replace generic claims with specific proof, useful information, or a more human statement.

### 2. Demonstrate expertise

Instead of: "Our team is highly trained."

Prefer: "Not every surface should be cleaned the same way. What works beautifully on one countertop can damage another." Then explain why.

Expertise should make the customer think: "I didn't know that." That's a powerful brand experience.

### 3. Explain our pricing instead of defending it

Never sound embarrassed about charging more.

We should be comfortable communicating: "We're not the cheapest cleaning company. We're not trying to be." Then explain the difference.

Our premium pricing should be connected to: experience, expertise, systems, consistency, reliability, accountability, quality control, customer communication, professional standards, the overall experience.

The goal is for the customer to conclude: "Now I understand why they cost more."

### 4. Use specificity

Generic: "We pay attention to the details."

Better: "We're the people who notice the stuff you weren't looking for." Or even better, when appropriate, describe the actual detail.

Specificity creates credibility.

### 5. Use humor strategically

Humor should: make the brand feel human; make a point memorable; break up a long section of copy; make the customer smile; highlight an observation people recognize.

Humor should NOT: exist just because we think we need a joke; distract from important information; make serious information seem unserious; make customers feel judged; make us sound inexperienced.

If the joke doesn't improve the communication, remove it.

### 6. Write like we're talking to ONE homeowner

Don't write: "Homeowners seeking reliable residential cleaning solutions can benefit from our comprehensive service offerings."

Write: "You shouldn't have to wonder whether your cleaning company is actually going to show up."

Speak directly to the individual. Use "you" and "we."

### 7. Don't oversell

We don't need to constantly say: best, premium, luxury, exceptional, superior, unmatched, unparalleled, elite, five-star.

If the website communicates our expertise, standards, process, and customer experience clearly, people should reach the conclusion themselves. Understatement can communicate confidence.

## The VCT Voice Test

Before publishing ANY copy, evaluate it against these questions:

1. Does this sound like a real person? If not, rewrite it.
2. Does this demonstrate expertise or merely claim expertise? If it only claims expertise, make it more specific.
3. Are we communicating our value without apologizing for our price? If not, revise.
4. Is the humor subtle? If it feels like we're trying to be funny, tone it down.
5. Is the customer the butt of the joke? If yes, remove it.
6. Does this sound like a generic cleaning company? If yes, rewrite.
7. Does this sound like a marketing agency wrote it? If yes, rewrite it.
8. Would we actually say this to a customer standing in their kitchen? If no, rewrite it.
9. Does this make the customer think "These people know what they're doing."? If no, consider whether the copy is doing enough.
10. Does this make the customer think "I like these people."? If yes, that's a bonus.

The ultimate goal: good VCT copy makes the customer think all three —

- "They know what they're doing."
- "They seem honest."
- "I like them."

> The Valley Clean Team sounds like the smartest person in the room who doesn't need everyone to know they're the smartest person in the room.

> We're not trying to make a cleaning company sound fun. We're trying to make an expert company sound human.
